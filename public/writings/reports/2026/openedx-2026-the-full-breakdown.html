The five shifts

1 · The platform is being rebuilt around skills, not courses

The structural story of the week, and bigger than the AI story. Axim named it as the growth bet, with two funded initiatives for the next 12–18 months: Learning Pathways and competency tracking. Both require breaking the platform's founding assumption — as OpenCraft put it, "the core platform is built around one assumption, that a course is the biggest unit of learning." Pathways is being built on the new Open edX core, deliberately not on the legacy platform. Talk 07

2 · Everyone is talking about AI; almost nobody has evidence yet

Roughly a third of the talks touched AI, but only two presented real numbers from real learners. The most careful of those — a 20,000-learner randomised test of AI-presented video — found no measurable difference at all. The other, WGU's embedded AI practice activities, found a genuine improvement but on a narrow slice of courses. Everything else was architecture, prototypes and intent.

3 · Assessment is the acknowledged crisis

WGU's position: standardized assessments are "increasingly easily gamed by AI", and you cannot bolt AI onto the old model. Four separate talks converged on the same conclusion — stop measuring recall in a snapshot, start gathering evidence continuously.

4 · Government is the fastest-growing vertical, arriving at national scale

Ethiopia (50 universities), Portugal (1.5M K-12 users), India (13M registrations), Israel (1.2M), Uruguay (~1 in 14 citizens). Government is 17% of detected sites and, combined with nonprofits, would be the largest single segment.

5 · Growth has flattened — and the community finally has data honest enough to say so

The most under-discussed finding of the week, and the subject of the next section.

The rest of the footprint picture

  • Course volume grew ~50% — roughly 40,000 advertised courses in 2024 to ~68,000 in 2025, led by higher ed and government.
  • The ecosystem is concentrating. 25 large multi-tenant instances account for 35% of the footprint and 50% of all courses.
  • ~60% of sites didn't upgrade at all last year. Teak is now the main migration target; the long-standing Nutmeg bottleneck finally cleared.
  • Geography: Western Europe and North America ≈ 50% of sites; Middle East (250) and East Asia (179) another 30%.

Axim's 145M learner figure is scraped from public data on roughly the 20 largest instances, and they're candid it's a floor. The voluntary telemetry registry has run two years, but ~80% of instances are on versions too old to include it.

The gaps that kept coming up

Six things the platform structurally lacks. These made the list because they surfaced independently across different talks — they're not one person's wishlist.

No blueprint courses — you can't run many copies of one course from a single master

A blueprint is a master course you stamp copies from, where the copies stay linked: change the master and the change flows down to all of them. Open edX has no version of this.

How people cope: ASU rebuilt it as Salesforce automation. They run 10–30 iterations of a single course per session, 80–1,500 students each, and said on stage they're "really looking forward to when Open edX has a true blueprint." It's also blocking a decision — they want to split Ethiopia's courses per university but won't, because "updating 50 versions of the courses was not on our lean team model."

No approval workflow for creating courses — nothing tracks who requested a course or who signed off

Who can request a course, who approves it, what state it's in (in development → beta → live), and who gets which role at each stage. Open edX has none of this. It's excellent at delivering a course and has nothing for the process that produces one.

How people cope: also Salesforce. ASU built a four-stage pipeline with rules baked in — an instructor can't get a course shell until they've passed the teaching course. With 50 universities and 35,000 professors, by hand isn't an option. "edX is great at delivering the course. But that intake and that approval process, not sure where that is."

You can't push a library update out to many courses — each course has to accept the change itself

Shared content libraries only work in one direction: pull. Edit a component in the library and every course using it must individually accept the update. You can't push one fix out to the 20 courses that need it.

How people cope: they don't. Asked directly at the libraries talk — "is there a way to push it out from the library so you don't have to go to say 20 courses?" — the answer was "not not yet." There's a real reason, not just missing work: someone publishing a change meant for one course could shove it into a course they don't even have permission to see.

Only quiz-type components can be graded — a decade-old complaint

The only thing the platform can put a score on is an XBlock — the plugin format every quiz, video and problem is built as. So a discussion, a peer review, or work done outside the platform can't be graded unless you wrap it in one.

How people cope: they wrap it in an XBlock. "We have been asked time and time again for the past over a decade now — can we grade discussions? And eventually it all ends up being 'oh well, yeah, I guess you can make an XBlock that holds that.'" The cause is a design decision: XBlock bundled scoring, display and storage together, so grading got permanently tied to it.

No built-in way to sell courses — the payments feature was removed, not never built

Open edX used to ship an e-commerce service. It was pulled after the shopping-cart library it was built on dropped support for MySQL, the database Open edX runs on. Taking payments is now entirely a third-party job.

How people cope: a WooCommerce plugin maintained by the community. Ethiopia built its local payment gateways on top of it, since PayPal doesn't operate there. Zite Labs is reportedly building a managed replacement.

No official accessibility report — nothing to hand a procurement team that asks

A VPAT is the standard document a vendor produces stating how its product measures up against accessibility rules. Procurement committees and regulators ask for it by name. Open edX doesn't have one.

How people cope: eduNEXT went looking, found nothing, and wrote an unofficial draft themselves against the WCAG 2.2 standard. This is getting urgent — the European Accessibility Act now wants proof, and good intentions are explicitly not a legal defence: "they are starting to ask you for evidence."

Notice Salesforce appears twice. That's the tell — customers are rebuilding core platform functions in a sales CRM because the platform doesn't offer them. Blueprints and the approval workflow are the two that fast-growing clients hit first.

Releases: Ulmo (Dec 2025) → Verawood (June 2026) → Willow (Dec 2026). An LTS on a two-year cadence is under discussion.

AI: separating real from narrated

Axim's official position is deliberately restrained. The argument for the LMS in an AI world is accountability — without the harness, "is there any learning actually happening? Or was it all a series of hallucinations that made the person feel good about what they already know?" Talk 05

Human presenter vs. AI avatar: no measurable difference

2U/edX ran a randomised A/B test in the "How to Learn Online" course. ~20,000 learners split evenly. Every learner got a 2-minute summary video at the end of each of four modules — identical script, but one group saw a human presenter and the other saw an AI avatar. This is what researchers call a null result: the thing being tested made no difference to the outcomes measured. Not a failed experiment — a finding.

But "no difference" is not the same as "nobody cared." Most free-text comments were neutral, yet a small and vocal group hated it: "This is AI slop." "This is so unsettling." "Stop taking away jobs from humans." That's a reputational signal that will never show up in a completion metric.

The team's own conclusion was explicitly not "ship avatars." Two reasons: this was supplementary content — a throwaway two-minute recap, not core teaching — and they haven't yet tested it on hard material like worked problems or emotionally loaded topics. What they recommend is running more experiments, and they published the method so you can. Talk 57 · 16:33

The best production evidence

WGU's in-flow AI — a guardrailed prompt embedded in the learning activity, not a general chatbot. Live in 84 courses of ~1,200, ~17,000 learners. Because the programme is competency-based, using the material is optional — and 80–100% opted in. First-attempt pass rate improved 18% on a gen-ed course with ~20k enrolment. Their own caveat is the useful one: "It's not AI plopped into a course. It's how you plop it in."

The honest limits, from the person who built the framework

"We have no idea how much this costs. If you have 1,000 users, how much is my token count going to run? The first one who gets 1,000 users here can tell me."

Felipe Montoya on AI Extensions — performance at scale untested, no token or quota management, real provider inconsistencies around tool-calling. His ask was blunt: "The largest pilot so far was yesterday at the tutorials." Talk 14

Where AI is doing genuinely novel work

  • Synthetic students — decomposing a learner into ~7 small models rather than one LLM roleplaying. "A generalist model pretending to be four specialists is not four specialists." The structure agent caught an ontology bug: module 2 asks students to read vitamin labels; module 3 teaches what vitamins are. Talk 53
  • Continuous assessment — cognitive task analysis → domain model → learner cognition model → just-in-time activities and rubrics, with automated cut scores. Deliberately offloads to small models and plain Python for predictability. Talk 58
  • Agentic course production — Santander's ~100 small agents turning market signals into courses and clips. On cost: "I don't think we should put costs before exploration." Talk 21
  • AI grading is harder than it looks — sycophancy is structural: when uncertain, the model completes toward the most likely token, and the most likely answer is the correct one, so it silently upgrades wrong student answers. Talk 35

Skills & credentials

Unicon interviewed 10 institutions running CBE on Canvas, Brightspace and Blackboard. The verbatim from Calbright is the thesis statement:

"There's no clean system. Institutions are just MacGyvering things together." Talk 12 · 7:13

Every institution named the same #1 gap — progress tracking, done in manual spreadsheets. Skills get tagged but can't drive what happens next. CBE roles (coach, mentor, advisor) don't exist in any LMS role model.

The credentials stack has stabilized: W3C Verifiable Credentials → Open Badges 3.0. Good metaphor from DCC: "if a verifiable credential is a car, an Open Badge is a school bus." Blockchain was quietly dropped — it was the wrong layer.

But the employer loop is not closed, and they said so. Employers are interested but "have no way to really consume verifiable credentials" — ATS and HRIS vendors haven't adopted the schemas. The reframe offered: the near-term value of learning records is learner confidence and visibility, not employer verification. Talk 10

The one hard outcome number surfaced in a hallway an hour before the talk: veterans holding Education Design Lab durable-skills credentials entered jobs earning $10,000 more on average than their same-cohort peers. The usual gender gap inverted — women gained substantially more.

The arguments the community is having

Flexibility is an asset in higher ed and a liability in corporate training

From hundreds of sales meetings: professional-training buyers like Open edX, get excited, then stall — never on capability. The recurring phrase was "powerful but risky." Discovery, which higher ed reads as diligence, reads to them as unbounded scope. Same product, different framing: "highly customizable open-source LMS" loses; "enterprise learning platform with open-source flexibility" wins. And this market is larger and growing faster than higher ed. Talk 32

Contribute vs. fork, with receipts

Contributors get 2× the productivity benefit of free riders; a private fork costs the average company ~5,000 hours per release cycle, roughly $250k. Read that one carefully: it comes from a 560-respondent Linux Foundation survey across open source generally, not Open edX — and it is per release cycle, not per year. Open edX ships twice a year, and the figure scales with company size and fork count. OpenCraft's cautionary tales: McKinsey Academy died at 6–9 months of reconciliation work per release; FUN, France's first government instance, has been frozen for ~10 years. But they concede that for every quote they made, two or three providers quoted a tenth of the price.

Upgrade debt is the biggest silent liability

68% of tracked sites — 795 of them — didn't move a single release in a year. That's the diagonal of a year-on-year migration matrix, not one shared version: the biggest clusters are Nutmeg (139 sites) and Juniper/Koa (102), with some Hawthorn instances six or seven years old still in production. Those stranded sites hold roughly half of all course content, and 90% are still operational. For contrast, 236 sites moved Lilac → Redwood and 162 moved Redwood → Teak. The real forcing function is that security patches only ship for the latest release. Advice: upgrade once a year, skipping one release; "latest stable" means a few months after release, not release week. Talk 44

Accessibility: intent is no longer a defense

The European Accessibility Act wants VPATs and evidence, not diversity statements. There is no official Open edX VPAT — eduNEXT published an unofficial first draft. The live screen-reader demo found real bugs: first heading is h3 not h1; a graph whose alt text reads "graphic"; drag-and-drop that throws focus to the top of the page — brutal in a timed exam; and in Studio, edit fields announced as buttons with no confirmation that Save worked. When 2U bought edX in 2021, Harvard and MIT kept the open-source code and sold only the brand, website and commercial assets. The sale proceeds funded Axim, and the code became its responsibility. That's why your upstream is Axim and not 2U.

The critical thing to understand is the funding model: Axim lives on a one-time endowment, not revenue from the platform. It has no product to sell you.

Why it matters to you That endowment model explains the whole operating style — they fund initiatives rather than ship features, the community does most of the building, and "contribute back" is pitched as ecosystem survival rather than charity. Their two funded bets for the next 12–18 months are Learning Pathways and competency tracking, so those are the defaults you'll inherit.

The founding detail explains everything about them: Leavitt came from technology and wanted to know what the internet could do for access; Romer came from the Air Force, where training is already competency-based. They imported that model wholesale. Students progress by demonstrating mastery, never by seat time.

Structurally unusual in two ways. First, a specialized faculty model — separate people build curriculum, teach, and evaluate, and the evaluators grade against rubrics without ever seeing the student's name. They assessed ~3 million performance submissions last year. Second, every student gets a dedicated mentor; on the student panel that mattered far more to people than any technology.

They hold themselves to three results — completion, return (income change within two years), and equity. Gallup reports ~$15,000 average income lift within a year of graduating, with 97% of employers saying graduates are prepared.

Why it matters to you WGU is competency-based and Open edX is course-shaped. That mismatch is the engine behind the entire skills-not-courses rebuild. Rather than work around it they put engineers on moving it — instructor dashboard, RBAC, frontend-base — and Axim's roadmap now follows that pressure. Their requirements are quietly setting your future defaults.

ASU / EdPlus — Arizona State's digital arm, operating under a charter that measures the university "not by whom we exclude but by whom we include." How to read this

The jump button goes to the exact second — not to the top of the video

Every row's source chip is a YouTube link with a timestamp baked in. It lands on the moment that person is introduced or starts speaking — usually the sentence where they say their own name and job title.

Where the timestamps came from: I rebuilt each transcript from the raw caption files, which carry a start time on every line. The text you'd read in a plain transcript has that timing stripped out; going back to the caption source put it back.

Hover any underlined organisation name — you get a one-line explanation of what it is

Roughly forty organisations appear here and most of them are not household names. Where a name is underlined with a dashed line, hovering it — or tabbing to it and pressing nothing at all — shows a plain-language line on who they are and what they do in this ecosystem. Escape dismisses it.

One row per appearance, not per person — several people spoke at three or more sessions

Jenna Makowski of Axim appears five times and Felipe Montoya of eduNEXT four. Five more people — Stephanie Khurana, Laura, Arlena Mangan, Elizabeth Gordon and Ildi — appear three times each. Searching a name pulls up every session they were in, which is usually the more useful view.

Roles are grouped into eight buckets for filtering — the exact job title is still shown on every row

There are 111 distinct job titles in here, which is far too many to put in a dropdown. The filter groups them into eight — executive and strategy, engineering, product and design, learning design, research and data, business and partnerships, government and public sector, students and alumni — while the row itself always shows the title as the person gave it.

Some speakers are never named out loud — those rows say so instead of guessing

Six presenters introduce themselves as "I work at X" without ever saying their name, and the captions have no speaker labels. Rather than infer a name from context, those rows show the role and organisation and are marked as unnamed. They still filter and link like any other row.

Name spellings come from automatic captions — they are best-effort, and a few are visibly mangled

YouTube's speech recognition is unreliable on proper nouns, especially names it hasn't heard before. "Stephanie Khurana" is transcribed as "Stephanie Corrona"; "Zelalem Assefa" appears as "Zal Alemayehu Safa". I corrected the ones I could confirm from repeated mentions across sessions and left the rest as heard.

What that means for you: treat the spelling as a strong hint, not a citation. The jump link is the authoritative record — click it and you can hear the name.

Method & accuracy

Synthesized from all 57 conference transcripts. Timestamps were resolved by matching phrases against the caption files, so links land within a few seconds of the claim — usually a beat before it, so you hear the run-up. The one deliberate exception is the ranked watch-list: those recommend a whole talk, so they open at the beginning. Chart colours are the validated data-viz palette, not the page accent — the petrol used for links fails the chroma floor (0.078) and would read as grey in a chart.

Names came from auto-generated captions and several were mangled; corrections confirmed against employer: Stephanie Khurana, Courtney Hills McBeth, Kerri Lemoie, Xavier Antoviaque. Profile links appear only where both name and employer were verified. In the vertical chart, higher-ed and corporate counts were stated directly; government was given as a percentage and the remainder is derived.
